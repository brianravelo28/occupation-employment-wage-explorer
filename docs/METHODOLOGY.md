# Methodology

Abbreviations used below: BLS (Bureau of Labor Statistics), OEWS (Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics), JOLTS (Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey), CPI-U (Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers), NAICS (North American Industry Classification System, the industry codes), SOC (Standard Occupational Classification, the occupation codes), YoY (year over year) and IQR (interquartile range: the middle 50% of values).

## Data sources and constraints

OEWS is annual, and JOLTS has no occupation breakdown. Both facts shape how the project is built.

- **OEWS is annual, not monthly**, and BLS does not retain OEWS history under stable series IDs in its timeseries API: every series has `begin_year == end_year` (the current release only). The historical panel comes from BLS's separate annual archive files (`oesm{YY}all.zip`, one per year, published at bls.gov/oes/special-requests/), which this project downloads and stacks itself (`data/fetch_oews.py`). The reference period is May of each year, and the panel covers the 2015–2025 releases.
- **JOLTS has no occupation-level breakdown.** Series are keyed by NAICS industry only, and BLS publishes industry detail for the US but, at the state level, only Florida *total nonfarm* (queried 2026-10-12: Florida retail, leisure & hospitality, health care, construction and manufacturing all return "Series does not exist"). `data/fetch_jolts.py` pulls the US industry series and Florida total nonfarm, and the industry rates are attached to occupations by employment-weighting (see [CPI and JOLTS](#cpi-and-jolts)).
- **OEWS column names and casing drift across release years** (2015 uses `"occ code"` with a space and truncates the group label to `"detail "`; 2019 uses `occ_code`/`o_group`; 2024 uses `OCC_CODE`/`O_GROUP`; the `PRIM_STATE` column doesn't exist before about 2020). The parser normalizes headers and identifies Florida with the state code (`AREA_TYPE == 2 & AREA == 12`, Florida's FIPS code), which is stable across every release year. "Florida" throughout this project means the state as a whole; no metro or county rows are used.

## CPI and JOLTS

**Real wages (CPI-U).** OEWS wages describe a May reference period, so each release year's mean annual wage is deflated with that year's May CPI-U (all items, US city average, not seasonally adjusted) and expressed in dollars of the latest release year (currently 2025). `wage_annual_mean_real` is the result, `real_wage_growth_yoy_pct` is `(1 + nominal growth) × CPI(May of prior year) ÷ CPI(May of this year) − 1`, and `purchasing_power_loss_flag` is 1 when the nominal wage rose but the real wage fell. Adding a newer release changes the base year, so rerun the feature builders afterwards. The CPI series has no October 2025 value; this does not affect the build because wages are deflated using May values only. OEWS wages are themselves updated to the reference period by BLS, so the deflation is approximate, not exact.

What it shows: the share of US occupations whose real wage fell was 19% (2016), 40%, 63%, 34%, 7% (2020), **81% (2021), 85% (2022)**, 32%, 40%, 39% (2025). Across 2015–2025 the median US occupation's real wage rose 0.8% in total, and 46% of the 713 occupations present in both years ended lower.

**JOLTS rates and "tightness".** JOLTS reports each industry's job openings rate (openings as a share of employment plus openings), quits rate and layoffs-and-discharges rate. The dashboard calls these job-market tightness: high openings and quits suggest workers are in demand, high layoffs the opposite.

**JOLTS exposure.** JOLTS has no occupation dimension, so each occupation gets an *exposure* rate: its industries' annual-average rates weighted by where its employment sits in the national occupation × sector OEWS panel (`src/build_macro_features.py`). OEWS sectors map to the closest JOLTS industry (wholesale trade takes the broader trade, transportation and utilities rate; agriculture has none because JOLTS is nonfarm). Three rates are used (openings, quits, layoffs and discharges), averaged over the calendar year of the OEWS release. Florida occupations use the same national industry rates, because BLS publishes no Florida industry detail, and the Florida total-nonfarm openings rate enters separately as a regional figure (`jolts_region_openings_rate`). Each sector row in the industry table also carries its own sector's three rates. The JOLTS series run through August 2026 for the US and December 2025 for Florida (state data lag); only calendar years up to 2025 are used, so the difference does not affect any figure.

## 2020 shock and recovery

The fourth tab describes the one large shock the data contain. It makes no projections.

**Method** (`src/build_shock_replay.py`). For each occupation and region, the *response* is its employment change in the 2020 OEWS release minus its average change over the 2016–2019 releases (at least two of them), in percentage points. Subtracting the earlier trend means only the part plausibly due to the shock remains: an occupation growing 3% a year that fell 5% in 2020 has a response of −8 points, not −5. The tab also shows employment indexed to the 2019 release (= 100), set against the total for a fixed set of occupations: those with reported employment in every release. 700 US occupation codes appear in all 11 years and 699 of them also have reported employment every year, so the US comparison set has 699 occupations; for Florida the figures are 619 and 536. Because the set is fixed, the line cannot move just because occupation codes were added or dropped.

**Small occupations.** A single occupation's YoY change is very noisy below about 10,000 employees, so an occupation needs at least 10,000 employees in both 2019 and 2020 to show its own response. Smaller ones, and occupations with too little pre-2020 history, show their SOC major group's response (the group's total employment change, same trend adjustment), and the tab says which was used. For 2025 employment, 79% of US workers and 68% of Florida workers are in occupations that show their own response. The "hardest hit" and "most resilient" tables list only occupations with their own response and leave out the "All Other" residual categories, which do not describe a single job. The hardest-hit end is clear (personal services, entertainment and food service dominate); at the resilient end, responses of a few points are within sampling noise.

**What it shows.** Nationally, total employment in the 2020 release fell 5.3% (Florida 4.2%), against a pre-2020 trend of about +1.6% a year. Responses are very uneven: Waiters and Waitresses −25.3 points, Retail Salespersons −13.6, Cashiers −8.2, Registered Nurses −2.0. For the fixed comparison set, total employment in 2025 is 103.3% of its 2019 level nationally and 110.5% in Florida.

**Limits.** It is one episode, and a different shock would hit different occupations. Each OEWS release pools three years of survey panels, so the 2020 release shows the shock smeared over time, and the rebound arrives mostly in the 2022 release. An occupation that did not exist under the same code in 2019 (for example Software Developers, whose code changed) has no 2019 base and is not shown. The response of a small occupation's group may not fit that occupation.

## Florida vs. national wages

All figures use 2025 OEWS mean annual wages, in nominal dollars (not adjusted for cost of living).

**The headline: about 8% less for the same job.** Compared head-to-head (Florida wage ÷ US wage for each occupation, over the 777 occupations with a wage in both regions), the median gap is **−7.7%** (25th–75th percentile −13.5% to −2.5%), and **84%** of those occupations pay less in Florida. Weighting each occupation's gap by its Florida employment gives **−6.5%**.

**Why the Regional Comparison chart implies about 11%.** The tab's wage chart compares each region's middle occupation: the median across the 790 occupations present in both regions of each occupation's mean annual wage, $58,730 in Florida against $66,290 nationally, a **−11.4%** gap. That is the gap between two distribution medians, which is not the same thing as the median of the per-occupation gaps, so use the 8% figure for "how much less does the same job pay in Florida".

**Counting workers instead of occupations.** With "each worker counts equally" selected, every occupation is weighted by its own employment in that region (US weights for the US, Florida weights for Florida), so Retail Salespersons counts for millions and a 500-person occupation for 500. The median occupation-mean wage then becomes **$53,781 nationally against $50,391 in Florida, a −6.3% gap**. These are medians of occupation-level numbers, not of individual workers' pay.

**How much to trust Florida's figures.** Florida's median comparable occupation has 2,615 employees (the national median is 47,995), and 32% of its 790 occupations have fewer than 1,000 (none of the national ones do, bar one). In 2025 the standard deviation of YoY employment growth was 59 points for Florida occupations under 1,000 employees against 12 points for those over 10,000. Small cells are where OEWS sampling noise and carried-forward estimates concentrate, so treat Florida's figures, growth and contraction in particular, as less certain than the national ones.

**What this does not show.** These are nominal wages. Prices differ between places, so the gap says nothing about purchasing power, and a regional price index is not part of this build.

### The other Regional Comparison charts: occupation view and worker view

Counting occupations equally lets noisy small Florida cells dominate Florida's figures, while weighting by workers lets large, steadier occupations dominate. Neither view is the "right" one: the occupation view describes the breadth of the job market, the worker view describes what the typical worker faces. In 2025 the two views give:

| | Occupation view (US / Florida) | Worker view (US / Florida) |
|---|---|---|
| Median employment growth | +0.4% / −0.3% | +1.0% / +2.1% |
| Share contracting (employment down more than 2%) | 38% / 46% | 22% / 32% |
| Share with a nominal wage decline | 12% / 29% | 6% / 17% |
| Share with a real wage decline | 39% / 45% | 38% / 43% |

## Known limitations

- **Annual, not monthly.** Trend charts move once a year, not continuously.
- **Suppressed values stay blank.** BLS suppresses some employment and wage estimates (marked `*` or `#`); the pipeline keeps them as blanks, never zeros. Growth, real-wage and JOLTS-exposure columns are blank wherever one of their inputs is, charts skip those points, and the 2020 tab's fixed comparison set keeps only occupations with reported employment in every release. About 1.6% of employment values and 1.0% of mean wages are blank (see [SCHEMA.md](SCHEMA.md)), and the build scripts print how many rows lack each derived value.
- **OEWS isn't designed for year-over-year comparison.** BLS's FAQ says the survey produces cross-sectional estimates and is "less useful for looking at changes over time", citing classification changes, methodology changes, and its three-year pooled sample design ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/oes/oes_ques.htm)). Each release combines six semiannual panels collected over three years; the May 2025 estimates use May 2025, Nov 2024, May 2024, Nov 2023, May 2023 and Nov 2022, so consecutive releases share four of their six panels. YoY changes here are therefore changes in a moving average, and every growth rate in this project inherits that: read them as directional.
- **Occupation codes change between releases.** US codes went from 808 to 789 between 2018 and 2019 (98 dropped, 79 new) and 30 were dropped and 72 added between 2020 and 2021. Only 700 US occupation codes appear in all 11 years. YoY changes are matched on the actual prior year, so they are blank where a year is missing.
- **Small occupations are noisy.** OEWS sampling error dominates in small occupations: the standard deviation of YoY employment growth (US and Florida, all years) is about 67 points for occupations under 1,000 employees, 21 for 1,000–10,000, 9 above 10,000 and 7 above 50,000. The ranked tables on the Industry & Sector tab therefore exclude occupations under 500 employees in either year compared (`SECTOR_RANKING_MIN_EMPLOYMENT` in `app/app.py`; for example a jump from 120 to 960 employees reads as +700%); the scatter still shows them, sized by employment. The notebook's national top-movers tables apply no floor.
- **Some estimates are carried forward unchanged.** For some smaller Florida occupations BLS publishes the identical employment figure in consecutive releases (2024 release: 13 of 744 comparable occupations against 2 nationally; 2025 release: 11 of 737 against 3 of 790). In 2024 that put Florida's median employment growth at exactly 0.0% (−0.25% in 2025). The Regional Comparison tab shows medians with 25th–75th percentile whiskers for this reason; they span the IQR rather than the full range because a few small occupations swing from about −82% to +300%.
- **Wages are reported as mean annual wages and their real change, not within-occupation dispersion.** The dashboard reports whether mean wages kept pace with inflation. It does not track whether pay gaps within an occupation (for example the 90th-percentile wage relative to the 10th) are widening or narrowing. In the 2015–2024 releases BLS does not publish median and percentile wages above a ceiling that rose over the period (the largest published 90th-percentile wage was about $187,000 in the 2015 release and $236,000 in 2024), so about 5% of occupation rows have no 90th-percentile wage, and a dispersion measure would drop the highest-paid occupations without warning. The 2025 release publishes them (5 US rows are blank). Mean wages are published for nearly all of these occupations, and the dashboard uses them.
- **Real wages are deflated with the national CPI-U for all regions.** BLS does not publish a state-level CPI, so Florida wages are adjusted with national inflation. Regional differences in real wage change therefore reflect nominal wage growth only; if Florida inflation diverged from the national rate, Florida's real wage change is over- or understated accordingly. The dashboard does not adjust for cost-of-living *level* differences between regions.
- **JOLTS is industry-level, not occupation-level.** JOLTS is published by industry only, and state data exists only for Florida total nonfarm. Occupation figures are therefore employment-weighted averages of industry rates, and Florida occupations use national industry rates. As a result, occupations with similar industry mixes receive near-identical values, and differences between occupations within the same industry are not captured. The Florida total-nonfarm openings rate is shown separately as a regional figure.
