# Methodology

Abbreviations used below: BLS (Bureau of Labor Statistics), OEWS (Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics), JOLTS (Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey), CPI-U (Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers), NAICS (North American Industry Classification System, the industry codes), SOC (Standard Occupational Classification, the occupation codes) and YoY (year over year).

## Terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Employment | The estimated number of **jobs** held by wage and salary workers, full-time and part-time, in nonfarm establishments, for each occupation. It excludes the self-employed, owners and partners in unincorporated firms, household workers, unpaid family workers and farm work. BLS rounds published employment to the nearest 10 (its field description in each data file). A total over occupations in this project is a total for that population, not total US employment. ([BLS: OEWS FAQ](https://www.bls.gov/oes/oes_ques.htm)) |
| Mean annual wage | The mean of straight-time gross pay across an occupation's jobs. Base rates, commissions, incentive pay, production bonuses, piece rates and tips are included; overtime, shift differentials, weekend and holiday premium pay, non-production bonuses and year-end bonuses are not; employer-paid benefits are not part of a wage. Annual wages assume a full-time, year-round schedule of 2,080 hours. ([BLS: OEWS FAQ](https://www.bls.gov/oes/oes_ques.htm), [technical notes](https://www.bls.gov/oes/2025/may/oes_tec.htm)) |
| Release year | The May reference period of an OEWS release. Each release pools six semiannual panels collected over three years, and BLS adjusts the wages collected in earlier panels to the May reference date (the MB3 method does this with aging factors computed from a wage regression model). |
| Real wage | The mean annual wage divided by May CPI-U, in 2025 dollars. |
| Real wage decline | An occupation's real wage is lower than in the previous release. Shares are counted over the occupations that have a mean wage in both releases, a number that changes by year (see [CPI and JOLTS](#cpi-and-jolts)). |
| Relative standard error (RSE) | BLS's percent relative standard error (PRSE): the standard error of an estimate as a percent of the estimate, which describes the error from estimating a figure from a survey of selected establishments instead of all of them. BLS's field description calls it sampling error in the 2015–2020 files and model error in the 2021–2025 files, with the same definition otherwise. BLS's MB3 statement says its standard errors cover sampling error only, not other biases ([BLS: MB3 statement](https://www.bls.gov/oes/mb3-methods.pdf)). BLS publishes it for employment and mean wage, not for medians or percentiles. |

## Data foundation

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Who collected it, and how | BLS runs OEWS as a survey of nonfarm establishments, drawn from state workforce agencies' unemployment-insurance lists and answered by mail, internet, email, telephone or in person. Each semiannual panel (May and November) samples about 186,000 to 189,000 establishments (179,000 to 187,000 in earlier panels). Employers report how many employees they have in each occupation, by wage range (12 ranges), not each worker's pay; BLS estimates mean wages from the ranges with a regression model. ([FAQ](https://www.bls.gov/oes/oes_ques.htm), [May 2025 technical notes](https://www.bls.gov/oes/2025/may/oes_tec.htm), [MB3 statement](https://www.bls.gov/oes/mb3-methods.pdf)) |
| Time coverage | A May reference period every year, 2015 to 2025: eleven releases, each pooling six semiannual panels collected over three years. The files carry no finer dates, so the project reports annual changes only. |
| Sampling | A probability sample stratified by state, substate area, industry, size and ownership. Three years of panels cover about 1.1 million establishments and about 57 percent of employment. The self-employed are outside the survey (see Employment in [Terms](#terms)). |
| Response | See the table below. |
| Quality and labels | BLS marks estimates it cannot publish as blank (`*`, `**`) and wages above a top-code as `#`; the project keeps blanks as blanks (see [Known limitations](#known-limitations)). BLS publishes a relative standard error for employment and mean wage (see [Terms](#terms)). |
| Real or synthetic | Real survey estimates. Nothing is simulated. |
| Licence | BLS content is in the public domain; BLS asks that it is cited as the source ([BLS copyright information](https://www.bls.gov/opub/copyright-information.htm)). The README and [CITATIONS.md](CITATIONS.md) cite BLS for every table. |
| Source choice | OEWS is BLS's source of employment and wage estimates for each detailed occupation, about 800 a year here, which is the unit the dashboard lets a reader look up. Other BLS sources answer parts of the question differently. The Employment Cost Index follows the cost of a fixed set of jobs, includes benefits and is published by occupational group and industry group ([ECI](https://www.bls.gov/eci/)); it fits "did pay for the same job keep up with prices" better than OEWS does, but not by detailed occupation. The Current Population Survey reports employment and weekly earnings by demographic group ([FAQ](https://www.bls.gov/oes/oes_ques.htm)). A rebuild on another source would change what a wage means. |

**Response rates.** Share of sampled establishments that responded, and the same weighted by sampled employment, for the six panels behind each year's estimates. BLS gives the 2015 to 2019 figures in its MB3 statement and the 2020 to 2025 figures in each year's technical notes; the two documents may define the rates slightly differently.

| Estimates | 2015 | 2016 | 2017 | 2018 | 2019 | 2020 | 2021 | 2022 | 2023 | 2024 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Establishments (%) | 73.5 | 72.8 | 72.0 | 71.2 | 70.9 | 69 | 67.2 | 65.4 | 65.8 | 65.7 | 66.2 |
| Employment (%) | 69.6 | 69.0 | 68.4 | 67.9 | 67.7 | 66 | 64.5 | 62.5 | 64.3 | 65.9 | 67.2 |

## Data sources and constraints

OEWS is annual, and JOLTS has no occupation breakdown. Both facts shape how the project is built. Everything is national: the project uses BLS's US estimates only.

- **OEWS is annual, not monthly**, and BLS does not retain OEWS history under stable series IDs in its timeseries API: every series has `begin_year == end_year` (the current release only). The historical panel comes from BLS's separate annual archive files (`oesm{YY}all.zip`, one per year, published at bls.gov/oes/special-requests/), which this project downloads and stacks itself (`data/fetch_oews.py`). The reference period is May of each year, and the panel covers the 2015–2025 releases.
- **BLS changed its estimation method in 2021, so 2015–2020 come from MB3 research estimates.** With the May 2021 estimates OEWS introduced a model-based method called MB3 ([BLS: May 2021 technical notes](https://www.bls.gov/oes/2021/may/oes_tec.htm)), and May 2022 was the second official release to use it ([BLS: May 2022 technical notes](https://www.bls.gov/oes/2022/may/oes_tec.htm)). For 2015–2020 BLS published estimates produced with MB3 as research estimates ([BLS: MB3 research estimates](https://www.bls.gov/oes/oes-mb3-methods.htm)); its survey methods and reliability statement says estimates produced with MB3 are directly comparable to its official estimates ([BLS: MB3 statement](https://www.bls.gov/oes/mb3-methods.pdf)), and the method is described in a [Monthly Labor Review article](https://www.bls.gov/opub/mlr/2019/article/model-based-estimates-for-the-occupational-employment-statistics-program.htm). The pipeline reads those files for 2015–2020 (`oes_mb3_{year}a.zip`) and the standard archives for 2021–2025, so all eleven years come from one method and no method change falls between the 2020 and 2021 releases. BLS labels the 2015–2020 files research estimates, so they can differ from the estimates published for those years at the time.
- **The raw files are recorded by checksum.** The processed tables were built from 17 BLS archives: the 11 standard OEWS archives (2015–2025) and the 6 MB3 research-estimate archives (2015–2020). Their SHA-256 values are in `data/raw_checksums.txt`. The archives themselves are not in the repo, and BLS can revise or replace a published file, so a later download may differ from the files used here. `python src/checksums.py` checks the files in `data/raw/` against the list, and `data/fetch_oews.py` reports each archive's status when it opens it (a difference is a warning, not an error).
- **JOLTS has no occupation-level breakdown.** Series are keyed by NAICS industry only. `data/fetch_jolts.py` pulls the 16 national industry series, and the industry rates are attached to occupations by employment-weighting (see [CPI and JOLTS](#cpi-and-jolts)).
- **OEWS column names and casing drift across release years** (2015 uses `"occ code"` with a space and truncates the group label to `"detail "`; 2019 uses `occ_code`/`o_group`; 2024 uses `OCC_CODE`/`O_GROUP`). The parser normalizes headers before extracting fields and keeps the national rows (`AREA_TYPE == 1`).

## How much the estimation method matters

The dashboard uses MB3 estimates for 2015–2020 (see [Data sources and constraints](#data-sources-and-constraints)). `src/mb3_comparison.py` measures what that choice changes by running the same feature code on a second series: the 2015–2020 estimates BLS first published (`data/processed/oews_occupation_panel_original_method.csv`, built by `data/fetch_oews_original.py`) joined to the same 2021–2025 estimates. The result is `data/processed/mb3_comparison.csv`.

- **Totals barely move.** The employment change summed over occupations differs by at most 0.2 points in any release, and the fixed comparison set's 2025 employment is 103.3% of its 2019 level with the original files and 103.3% with MB3.
- **The 2015–2020 levels differ from the figures BLS first published.** Across the 4,831 occupation-year rows for those years that have an employment value in both series, the median row's employment differs by 3.6% and its mean wage by 1.5%; 37% of rows differ by more than 5% in employment and 38% by more than 2% in mean wage. A reader who checks a 2015–2020 figure against BLS's published table will see these differences, and the dashboard says so beside its charts.
- **One occupation's figures move.** The median occupation's 2021 employment change differs by 4.0 points between the two series (correlation 0.70), and its 2021 real wage growth by 1.3 points. The differences are confined to 2021 and earlier: the 2022–2025 changes use only 2021–2025 files and are identical.
- **The early real-wage-decline shares move.** 31% (2016) and 51% (2017) with MB3, against 19% and 40% with the original files; from 2018 on they differ by at most 3.7 points. The 2021 share is 80.5% with the original files and 82.0% with MB3, and the 2022 share is 85% either way.
- **Individual 2020 responses move.** Waiters and Waitresses −25.3 with the original files and −29.9 with MB3; Registered Nurses −2.0 with the original files and −2.5 with MB3.

With the original files, the method change would fall between the 2020 and 2021 releases and sit inside every 2021 figure. With MB3 for all eleven years it does not.

## CPI and JOLTS

**Real wages (CPI-U).** OEWS wages describe a May reference period, so each release year's mean annual wage is deflated with that year's May CPI-U (all items, US city average, not seasonally adjusted) and expressed in dollars of the latest release year (currently 2025). `wage_annual_mean_real` is the result, `real_wage_growth_yoy_pct` is `(1 + nominal growth) × CPI(May of prior year) ÷ CPI(May of this year) − 1`, and `purchasing_power_loss_flag` is 1 when the nominal wage rose but the real wage fell. Adding a newer release changes the base year, so rerun the feature builders afterwards. The CPI series has no October 2025 value; this does not affect the build because wages are deflated using May values only. OEWS wages are pooled across three years and adjusted by BLS to the May reference date (see [Terms](#terms)), so the deflation is approximate, not exact.

What it shows: the share of occupations whose real wage fell was 31% (2016), 51%, 63%, 37%, 7% (2020), **82% (2021), 85% (2022)**, 32%, 40%, 39% (2025). Each share is taken over the occupations with a mean wage in both the current and the previous release: 755 of the 831 occupations in the 2021 release had a wage in both 2020 and 2021 and 824 of 830 in 2022 (new or recoded occupation codes have no prior year). Weighted by employment, 79% of jobs in 2021 and 86% in 2022 were in occupations whose real wage fell, and the median occupation's real wage changed by −2.2% in 2021 and −3.3% in 2022. Across 2015–2025 the unweighted median occupation's real wage rose 1.0% in total, and 45% of the 713 occupations present in both years ended lower.

**Sensitivity to the CPI month.** The deflator is May CPI-U because OEWS wages describe a May reference period. Using April, June, July or the annual-average CPI instead changes the shares (`src/robustness.py`). The 2022 decline holds under every choice (2022: 81–88%), and 2021 is a majority-decline year under every choice (2021: 73–84%), though its exact figure depends on the month. Later years are more sensitive (2023: 21–47%), so only the 2021 and 2022 findings are stated as headline results.

**Sensitivity to sampling error.** BLS publishes a relative standard error for each mean wage. Treating each wage as an estimate with that error (`real_decline_with_sampling_error` in `src/robustness.py`, with the two years' errors combined as if independent, which overstates the error of a change because consecutive releases share panels) gives an expected share with a decline of 79% in 2021 and 83% in 2022, close to the observed 82% and 85%. The decline is more than two standard errors from zero for 47% of occupations in 2021 and 60% in 2022; the rest are small changes that sampling error alone could reverse. The shares count occupations whose estimate fell; they are not a count of certain declines.

**What a real wage change measures.** An occupation's mean wage is a three-year pooled estimate whose earlier panels BLS has adjusted to the May reference date. Its year-to-year change therefore reflects that adjustment and any change in who works in the occupation (employers, areas, experience), not the pay change of a fixed job. A real wage decline here means the occupation's mean wage rose less than prices, not that a worker in that job was paid less in real terms. The Employment Cost Index (ECI) is built for the fixed-job question: BLS says it uses a fixed basket of labor, free of the effects of workers moving between occupations and industries, and it covers total compensation including benefits ([BLS: ECI](https://www.bls.gov/eci/), [Q&A](https://www.bls.gov/eci/questions-and-answers.htm)). BLS publishes the ECI by occupational group and industry group. This project uses OEWS because it gives employment and mean wages for each of about 800 detailed occupations in the same files, which is the unit the dashboard lets a reader look up.

**JOLTS rates and "tightness".** JOLTS reports each industry's job openings rate (openings as a share of employment plus openings), quits rate and layoffs-and-discharges rate. The dashboard calls these job-market tightness: high openings and quits suggest workers are in demand, high layoffs the opposite.

**JOLTS exposure.** JOLTS has no occupation dimension, so each occupation gets an *exposure* rate: its industries' annual-average rates weighted by where its employment sits in the national occupation × sector OEWS panel (`src/build_macro_features.py`). OEWS sectors map to the closest JOLTS industry (wholesale trade takes the broader trade, transportation and utilities rate; agriculture has none because JOLTS is nonfarm). Three rates are used (openings, quits, layoffs and discharges), averaged over the calendar year of the OEWS release. Each sector row in the industry table also carries its own sector's three rates. The JOLTS series run through August 2026; only calendar years up to 2025 are used, so the later months do not affect any figure.

## 2020 shock and recovery

The third tab describes the one large shock the data contain.

**Method** (`src/build_shock_replay.py`). For each occupation, the *response* is its employment change in the 2020 OEWS release minus its average change over the 2016–2019 releases (at least two of them), in percentage points. Subtracting the earlier trend leaves the change beyond what the occupation's own trend implies: an occupation growing 3% a year that fell 5% in 2020 has a response of −8 points, not −5. The tab also shows employment indexed to the 2019 release (= 100), set against the total for a fixed set of occupations: those with reported employment in every release. 700 occupation codes appear in all 11 years and 696 of them also have reported employment every year, so the comparison set has 696 occupations. Because the set is fixed, the line cannot move because occupation codes were added or dropped.

**Small occupations.** A single occupation's YoY change varies widely below about 10,000 employees, so an occupation needs at least 10,000 employees in both 2019 and 2020 to show its own response. Smaller ones, and occupations with too little pre-2020 history, show their SOC major group's response (the group's total employment change, same trend adjustment), and the tab says which was used. For 2025 employment, 79% of US workers are in occupations that show their own response. The "hardest hit" and "most resilient" tables list only occupations with their own response and leave out the "All Other" residual categories, which do not describe a single job. The hardest-hit end is clear (personal services, entertainment and food service dominate); at the resilient end, responses of a few points are within sampling noise.

**What it shows.** Employment summed over all occupations (see [Terms](#terms)) in the 2020 release fell 5.3%, against a pre-2020 trend of about +1.6% a year. Responses differ widely: Waiters and Waitresses −29.9 points, Retail Salespersons −14.9, Cashiers −7.7, Registered Nurses −2.5. For the fixed comparison set, total employment in 2025 is 103.3% of its 2019 level.

**How large is a response next to ordinary noise?** The same calculation applied to 2018 and 2019, years with no shock (their change minus the average of the earlier changes, same size rule), gives a standard deviation of 8.6 points, with 95% of those placebo responses between −16.8 and +18.5 points. 13% of the 580 occupations with their own 2020 response fall outside that range. A response inside it, including most of the resilient end of the table (7 of the 10 most resilient), cannot be told apart from ordinary variation. The placebo years have two or three earlier changes to average, against four for 2020, so their spread is slightly wide and the range is, if anything, conservative.

**Limits.** It is one episode, and a different event would show a different pattern across occupations. Each OEWS release pools three years of survey panels, so the 2020 release shows the 2020 change smeared over time, and the rebound arrives mostly in the 2022 release. An occupation that did not exist under the same code in 2019 (for example Software Developers, whose code changed) has no 2019 base and is not shown. The response of a small occupation's group may not fit that occupation.

## Known limitations

- **Annual, not monthly.** Trend charts move once a year, not continuously.
- **Suppressed values stay blank.** BLS suppresses some employment and wage estimates (marked `*` or `#`); the pipeline keeps them as blanks, never zeros. Growth, real-wage and JOLTS-exposure columns are blank wherever one of their inputs is, charts skip those points, and the 2020 tab's fixed comparison set keeps only occupations with reported employment in every release. 4 of 8,988 employment values and 0.6% of mean wages are blank (see [SCHEMA.md](SCHEMA.md)), and the build scripts print how many rows lack each derived value.
- **OEWS isn't designed for year-over-year comparison.** BLS's FAQ says the survey produces cross-sectional estimates and is "less useful for looking at changes over time", citing classification changes, methodology changes, and its three-year pooled sample design ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/oes/oes_ques.htm)). Each release combines six semiannual panels collected over three years; the May 2025 estimates use May 2025, Nov 2024, May 2024, Nov 2023, May 2023 and Nov 2022, so consecutive releases share four of their six panels. YoY changes here are therefore changes in a moving average, and every growth rate in this project inherits that: read them as directional.
- **Occupation codes change between releases.** US codes went from 808 to 789 between 2018 and 2019 (98 dropped, 79 new) and 30 were dropped and 72 added between 2020 and 2021. Only 700 occupation codes appear in all 11 years. YoY changes are matched on the actual prior year, so they are blank where a year is missing.
- **Small occupations are noisy.** OEWS sampling error dominates in small occupations: the standard deviation of YoY employment growth (all years) is about 36 points for occupations under 1,000 employees, 19 for 1,000–10,000, 9 above 10,000 and 7 above 50,000. The ranked tables on the Industry & Sector tab therefore exclude occupations under 500 employees in either year compared (`SECTOR_RANKING_MIN_EMPLOYMENT` in `app/app.py`; for example a jump from 120 to 960 employees reads as +700%); the scatter still shows them, sized by employment. The notebook's top-movers tables apply no floor.
- **BLS publishes a relative standard error for employment and mean wages.** The occupation card shows the relative standard error (RSE, as a percent of the estimate) next to the latest employment and mean wage, and the employment chart draws an approximate 95% range (the estimate plus or minus 1.96 standard errors). The median relative standard error of employment is 2.2%, and 4% of occupation-year rows have an employment RSE above 10%; the median for mean wages is 0.8%. BLS publishes no RSE for medians or percentiles. The band covers one release's relative standard error only; it does not account for the overlap between releases, so a change between two releases is less certain than the band suggests.
- **Some estimates are carried forward unchanged.** For a few occupations BLS publishes the identical employment figure in consecutive releases (among occupations with a reported value in both: 2024 release: 2 of 831; 2025 release: 3 of 830). That is too few to move national totals, but a growth rate of exactly 0.0% for a small occupation can be a repeated estimate rather than a flat labor market.
- **Wages are reported as mean annual wages and their real change, not within-occupation dispersion.** The dashboard reports whether mean wages kept pace with inflation. It does not track whether pay gaps within an occupation (for example the 90th-percentile wage relative to the 10th) are widening or narrowing. In the 2015–2024 releases BLS does not publish median and percentile wages above a ceiling that rose over the period (the largest published 90th-percentile wage was $186,000 in the 2015 release and $236,000 in 2024), so about 5% of occupation rows have no 90th-percentile wage, and a dispersion measure would drop the highest-paid occupations without warning. The 2025 release publishes them (5 rows are blank). Mean wages are published for nearly all of these occupations, and the dashboard uses them.
- **JOLTS is industry-level, not occupation-level.** JOLTS is published by industry only. Occupation figures are therefore employment-weighted averages of industry rates, so occupations with similar industry mixes receive near-identical values, and differences between occupations within the same industry are not captured.
